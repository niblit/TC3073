# Paper Card: HotFlip

**1. Full citation and link.**
> Ebrahimi, J., Rao, A., Lowd, D., & Dou, D. (2018). HotFlip: White-Box Adversarial Examples for Text Classification. *Proceedings of the 56th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Short Papers)*. [P18-2006](https://aclanthology.org/P18-2006/)

**2. What problem is studied?**
The paper investigates how to efficiently generate white-box adversarial examples to trick character-level (and word-level) neural text classifiers. It also explores using these generated examples to perform adversarial training, making models more robust against test-time attacks.

**3. What evidence or data is used?**
To evaluate character-level models, the authors use the AG's news dataset (120,000 training and 7,600 test instances) tested on a CharCNN-LSTM architecture. For word-level model evaluation, they use the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST) dataset tested on a Convolutional Neural Network (Kim's CNN). 

**4. What method is used?**
The authors introduce "HotFlip," a gradient-based optimization method. It uses the directional derivatives of the model's loss function with respect to the one-hot input vectors to estimate which single character change (flip, insert, or delete) will maximize the error. Because individual changes can be estimated efficiently, they utilize a beam search to string together multiple operations and find the worst-case adversarial combinations under a set budget.

**5. What are the two most important findings?**
*   **Finding 1:** Neural text classifiers are highly brittle; modifying an average of just 4.18% of the characters via beam search successfully tricked a character-level classifier over 90% of the time with high confidence.
*   **Finding 2:** Adversarial training using real adversarial examples generated by HotFlip significantly decreases misclassification error and improves the model's resilience far better than training on pseudo-adversarial examples (e.g., adding noise to embeddings).

**6. What is one important limitation?**
Generating insertion and deletion operations is highly computationally expensive (often $n$ times slower than a simple character flip), which forced the authors to restrict their adversarial training experiments solely to flip operations. Additionally, scaling this up to word-level attacks inherently risks changing the semantic meaning of the text, limiting the pool of viable adversarial examples once strict semantics-preserving constraints are applied.

**7. What will we use, change, test, or avoid because of this paper?**
> **Impact on our Adversarial Robustness Harness:** In week 4, our goal is to implement content-level transformations with a budget. We will **use** HotFlip's methodology for applying strict semantic constraints (such as part-of-speech matching and cosine similarity minimums) to guarantee that our word-level transformations remain intent-preserving, fulfilling our threat model's requirements. We will **test** our detector zoo's fragility by strictly limiting the perturbation budget to ensure attacks remain realistic, problem-space modifications rather than uncontrolled noise. Furthermore, we will **avoid** the assumption that character-level models are immune to token-level evasion, as HotFlip demonstrates their dense embedding spaces are highly sensitive to even minor perturbations.
